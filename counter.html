The typical company that lived on warrant paper lost value. The typical company that never touched it gained.

Same companies at both ends of the years on record, valued by the public float each one filed on its own annual report cover. Split by how many of their filings carried heavy warrant terms — price resets, inducements, cashless exercise, equity lines. The more of that paper a company filed, the more likely it was to end up worth less.

Correction — October 11, 2026. Until today this page showed a running total of about $16.8 trillion as “what holders of the warrant-paper companies that fell have lost.” That figure was wrong, and we have taken it down. Three reasons: (1) some companies’ filings report their float in the wrong units, and our filter let many of those errors through, which inflated every total (the starting value of the whole set came to $44 trillion, far above the real market); (2) it added up only the companies that fell, while the companies that rose in the same group gained more; and (3) it said “holders,” but public float includes funds and institutions, not just individual investors. The figures below are recomputed with each company checked against its own other years, every exclusion counted, and the typical (median) change shown, which one bad number cannot swing. The earlier figures remain viewable from the counter at ?screw_v1=1 for anyone who wants to compare.
