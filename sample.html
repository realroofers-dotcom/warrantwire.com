Customary adjustments

“subject to customary adjustments”

8-K summary language

Three words in the summary. The adjustments themselves are in the exhibit, and they are where the terms actually live. This phrase is the reason the exhibit gets read first on every job.

What we checked, and what we did not

Checks run

  • The exhibit read in full, not the 8-K summary of it
  • The inducement price against the market price on the date
  • Replacement warrant coverage, from the exhibit
  • The company's own accumulated deficit, from its 10-Q
  • Reverse splits on the record, with dates

Checks NOT run

  • What the authorized share count was left at after each split
  • Whether the registration statement had gone effective
  • Any private agreement not filed as an exhibit
  • Anything the company has not filed

Every read prints both lists. A finding that hides its own gaps is worth nothing the moment somebody finds the gap.

Five questions to ask this company

  1. How many shares are authorized today, and how many are outstanding?
  2. What was the authorized count left at after each reverse split?
  3. Who bought the replacement warrants, and are they the same parties as last time?
  4. What was paid to the placement agent, in cash and in warrants?
  5. What does the company expect to fund from here, and for how long?

Every read ends with five questions aimed at that company — because we may have missed something, and the holder often knows something the filing does not hold yet.
